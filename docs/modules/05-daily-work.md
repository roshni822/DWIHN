# AI in Daily DWIHN Work

<p class="module-lede">Useful AI work is more than generating an answer. A complete workflow includes permission, a trustworthy source, verification, correction, approval, and appropriate handling.</p>

## Module Overview

This module places AI inside complete workplace workflows for drafting, summarizing, reporting, reviewing, approving, and sharing. It is important because generating an output is only one step; the result must still be verified, corrected, approved, and handled properly.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module, learners can:

- Map a workplace task from source to approved destination.
- Select a small, repeatable task whose result can be checked.
- Review output for accuracy, completeness, tone, privacy, and approval.
- Measure usefulness using the complete task, including corrections.

### 1. Role-specific AI workflows

**Detailed Explanation:** An AI workflow connects a legitimate business need, approved source, permitted tool, clear instruction, human review, required approval, and authorized destination. The details differ by role, but human ownership remains constant.

**Key Points:**

- Begin with the business task, not with the AI tool.
- Define the source, owner, reviewer, and destination.
- Preserve all existing approval and professional responsibilities.

::: tip Real-life example
An administrator turns fictional meeting notes into an action summary. A program manager uses synthetic dashboard figures to draft a performance narrative. Each follows a different workflow but checks the result against its source.
:::

**Technical Example:** A workflow can be documented as ordered states: source received, permission confirmed, AI draft created, source comparison completed, reviewer approved, and final result stored in an authorized location.

**How It Works:**

1. Define the task and expected result.
2. Identify the approved source and tool.
3. Assign the employee and reviewer.
4. Generate and verify the output.
5. Approve, store, share, and monitor through existing processes.

**Where It Is Used:** Care coordination communication, administrative work, call-center documentation, program analysis, compliance drafts, and management reporting.

**Common Mistakes or Confusions:**

- Starting with a tool and searching for a task.
- Leaving the reviewer or output destination undefined.
- Assuming AI removes a required approval.

### 2. Writing emails, summaries, and reports

**Detailed Explanation:** AI can create a first draft when the employee supplies confirmed facts and a clear format. The employee should preserve decisions, responsibilities, open questions, and limitations rather than focusing only on polished language.

**Key Points:**

- Supply confirmed facts and a defined audience.
- Ask for a structure that can be checked.
- Preserve uncertainty and do not invent missing details.

::: tip Real-life example
A training reminder must include the confirmed time and optional attendance. The draft is rejected when it adds an unconfirmed room number, even though the rest of the email sounds professional.
:::

**Technical Example:** A report prompt may require fixed sections such as period, measures, observations, limitations, actions, and owners. Structured sections reduce the chance that essential information is silently omitted.

**How It Works:**

1. Gather the approved facts or notes.
2. Specify the audience, purpose, and output structure.
3. Generate the first draft.
4. Compare names, dates, figures, decisions, and actions with the source.
5. Revise and send through the normal review process.

**Where It Is Used:** Training reminders, internal memos, meeting summaries, weekly reports, policy drafts, public-information drafts, and leadership updates.

**Common Mistakes or Confusions:**

- Asking AI to fill gaps with reasonable assumptions.
- Preserving polished sentences while losing open questions.
- Sending the first draft without source comparison.

### 3. Reviewing and editing AI output

**Detailed Explanation:** Review accuracy, completeness, tone, accessibility, privacy, and approval. Check names, dates, numbers, commitments, and factual claims against the source. Correcting grammar alone is not enough.

**Key Points:**

- Review both what is present and what is missing.
- Treat every factual statement as a claim that needs support.
- Check whether the final output is appropriate for its audience and destination.

::: tip Real-life example
A meeting summary captures all decisions but omits an unresolved staffing question. The reviewer restores the question because leaving it out would give readers a false impression that the issue was settled.
:::

**Technical Example:** A review interface may label statements as supported, needs editing, unsupported, or missing. These statuses make the review decision visible and auditable.

**How It Works:**

1. Place the source beside the AI output.
2. Verify factual claims and calculations.
3. Find missing decisions, actions, owners, or limitations.
4. Check tone, readability, privacy, recipients, and approval.
5. Correct, reject, or escalate the output.

**Where It Is Used:** Every AI-generated draft, summary, chart narrative, call record, recommendation, translation, and exported report.

**Common Mistakes or Confusions:**

- Reviewing only spelling and tone.
- Correcting visible errors without checking omissions.
- Assuming a human-edited output automatically used a permitted workflow.

### 4. The daily AI checklist

**Detailed Explanation:** Before use, confirm permission and prepare the source. During use, provide clear instructions and avoid unnecessary information. After use, verify the output, obtain approval, and store or share it through the authorized route.

**Key Points:**

- Use the checklist before, during, and after the AI step.
- A safe input does not guarantee an accurate output.
- Complete the workflow only after approval and correct handling.

::: tip Real-life example
Before sending a drafted memo, the employee checks the organizational account, verifies every claim, confirms the intended recipients, obtains the normal review, and saves the final version in the approved location.
:::

**Technical Example:** A digital checklist can require confirmation of tool, account, data class, task, source, reviewer, and destination before the workflow status changes to complete.

**How It Works:**

1. Before: confirm permission, purpose, source, and minimum information.
2. During: use clear instructions and watch for unexpected behavior.
3. After: verify the output and correct errors.
4. Obtain the required approval.
5. Store, share, measure, and report through authorized routes.

**Where It Is Used:** Daily drafting, summarization, analytics, meeting support, call documentation, and any repeated AI-assisted task.

**Common Mistakes or Confusions:**

- Using the checklist only after a problem occurs.
- Checking input permission but not output handling.
- Marking the task complete before review and approval.

## Start with suitable tasks

A good first task is frequent, low-risk, narrow, and easy to verify. It has a known source, a clear owner, and an existing approval process.

<div class="concept-grid">
  <div class="concept-card"><strong>Good starting point</strong><p>Draft a routine reminder from approved public or fictional facts.</p></div>
  <div class="concept-card"><strong>Good starting point</strong><p>Organize fictional meeting notes into a required structure.</p></div>
  <div class="concept-card"><strong>Needs careful approval</strong><p>Summarize information that may contain sensitive details.</p></div>
  <div class="concept-card"><strong>Not an AI handoff</strong><p>Make a care, eligibility, crisis, or disciplinary decision.</p></div>

## Story: the missing open question

Luis asks an approved tool to summarize fictional meeting notes. The summary captures the decisions but omits an unresolved staffing question and assigns Friday as a deadline even though the group did not choose a date.

Luis compares the summary with the source, restores the open question, removes the unsupported deadline, and sends the corrected version through the normal approval process.

## The five-point review

### 1. Accuracy

Does every important statement match the source? Are names, dates, figures, and decisions correct?

### 2. Completeness

Did the output preserve required decisions, actions, owners, limitations, and unresolved questions?

### 3. Tone and accessibility

Is the language respectful, clear, appropriate for the audience, and free from unexplained jargon?

### 4. Privacy and security

Does the output contain sensitive information? Are the recipients, storage location, and sharing method authorized?

### 5. Approval

Who must review the result before it is used? Has AI changed any existing responsibility or sign-off? It should not.

## Interactive demonstration

Display fictional source notes beside an AI summary. Highlight each sentence using four labels:

- **Correct:** supported and complete.
- **Needs editing:** supported but unclear or poorly expressed.
- **Unsupported:** not established by the source.
- **Missing:** required information was omitted.

## Measuring whether AI helped

Measure the entire task, not only the seconds required to generate the first result.

<div class="workflow">
  <div class="workflow-step"><b>Prepare</b><span>Time to check permission and gather the source.</span></div>
  <div class="workflow-step"><b>Generate</b><span>Time to write the prompt and receive the result.</span></div>
  <div class="workflow-step"><b>Review</b><span>Time to verify, correct, and format the output.</span></div>
  <div class="workflow-step"><b>Approve</b><span>Time and effort in the existing review process.</span></div>
</div>

Useful measures include total completion time, number of corrections, critical error rate, readability, and whether the final result met the same quality standard as the existing process.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing a complex or high-consequence task as the first use case.
- Treating generation as task completion.
- Checking grammar but not facts.
- Losing decisions or uncertainty during summarization.
- Skipping the existing approval step.
- Storing or sharing the result outside the authorized route.
- Claiming time savings without counting review and corrections.

## Ethical consideration

Efficiency does not justify a lower standard of care, accuracy, accessibility, or privacy. If an AI-supported process creates more errors or disadvantages a group, the workflow must be changed or stopped.

## Mini challenge

The output is accurate and well written, but it was generated from a source that was not permitted in the tool. Can the output still be used?

::: details Suggested response
Do not assume the accurate output makes the workflow acceptable. Follow DWIHN's confirmed privacy or incident process and wait for direction on handling the output.
:::
