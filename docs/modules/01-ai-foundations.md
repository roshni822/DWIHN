# What AI Is and Is Not

<p class="module-lede">AI can help people draft, organize, compare, and analyze information. It can also produce convincing mistakes. This module builds a realistic mental model before learners use any tool.</p>

## Module Overview

This module introduces AI in plain language and establishes the most important course habit: treat AI as an assistant whose work must be checked. It matters because staff need a realistic understanding of AI before they can decide when it is useful, when it is unsafe, and what responsibility remains with the employee.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module, learners can:

- Explain AI in plain language without presenting it as a person or expert.
- Distinguish generative AI, analytics AI, and rules-based automation.
- Identify tasks that are appropriate, inappropriate, or require approval.
- Recognize unsupported content and explain why human review is necessary.

### 1. AI as a capable assistant

**Detailed Explanation:** AI is best understood as a tool that supports part of a person's work. It can organize information, create a first draft, find patterns, or suggest a structure. It does not understand a member's complete situation, hold professional responsibility, or replace an employee's judgment.

**Key Points:**

- AI supports a task; it does not own the task.
- AI is software, not a person, colleague, or licensed professional.
- The employee remains responsible for the final result and decision.

::: tip Real-life example
A program assistant uses an approved AI tool to turn five confirmed training details into a reminder email. The assistant still checks the date, time, audience, and registration link before sending it.
:::

**Technical Example:** A simple AI workflow receives a prompt and permitted facts as input, processes patterns learned during training, and returns a likely response. The response is a generated result, not a verified record.

**How It Works:**

1. A person defines the task.
2. The person checks whether the tool and information are permitted.
3. The tool processes the instruction and produces an output.
4. The person compares the output with the source.
5. An authorized person approves, changes, or rejects the result.

**Where It Is Used:** Drafting communication, organizing notes, suggesting report structures, supporting data exploration, and preparing first versions of routine material.

**Common Mistakes or Confusions:**

- Thinking AI understands the full situation like a person.
- Treating a fluent answer as a verified fact.
- Allowing AI to make a decision that belongs to an employee.

### 2. What AI can help with

**Detailed Explanation:** Useful beginner tasks include drafting routine communication, summarizing supplied material, simplifying general information, organizing notes, comparing text, and exploring governed data. These tasks work well when the source is clear and the result can be checked.

**Key Points:**

- Start with narrow, repeatable, low-risk tasks.
- Use a reliable source and a clear output format.
- Select tasks where errors and omissions can be found.

::: tip Real-life example
A manager has fictional meeting notes containing decisions, actions, and open questions. AI organizes the notes under those three headings, and the manager compares every item with the original notes.
:::

**Technical Example:** A summarization request may specify input text and a structured output such as Decisions, Actions, Owners, and Open Questions. The structure makes comparison with the source easier.

**How It Works:**

1. Gather the approved source.
2. Choose one clearly defined task.
3. Give the AI the task, context, format, and limits.
4. Review every important statement.
5. Correct the result and follow normal approval.

**Where It Is Used:** Internal reminders, fictional training cases, meeting summaries, first-draft reports, plain-language rewrites, and governed analytics questions.

**Common Mistakes or Confusions:**

- Selecting a complex task with no easy verification method.
- Providing more information than the task needs.
- Measuring only generation time and ignoring review time.

### 3. What AI should not be used for

**Detailed Explanation:** AI should not make final decisions about care, eligibility, crisis response, diagnosis, discipline, or another high-consequence matter requiring authorized professional judgment. It should also not receive information that is prohibited for the selected tool or account.

**Key Points:**

- High-consequence decisions require authorized human judgment.
- Approved tool use still depends on the account, information, and task.
- Existing clinical, legal, compliance, and escalation procedures remain in force.

::: tip Real-life example
A care coordinator may use an approved workflow to draft a general follow-up message from permitted facts. The coordinator must not ask AI to determine whether the fictional member qualifies for a service.
:::

**Technical Example:** A system may return a recommendation score, but application logic should not automatically convert that score into an eligibility decision without approved rules, review, and accountability.

**How It Works:**

1. Identify the decision being requested.
2. Determine whether it requires professional authority or creates significant consequences.
3. Check the approved workflow and responsible role.
4. Use AI only for an allowed supporting step, if any.
5. Keep the final judgment and documentation with the authorized person.

**Where It Is Used:** This boundary applies to care decisions, eligibility, crisis response, diagnosis, discipline, legal interpretation, and other high-impact processes.

**Common Mistakes or Confusions:**

- Believing AI becomes qualified when assigned an expert role in a prompt.
- Assuming a recommendation is neutral or correct.
- Bypassing an existing approval because the result appears confident.

### 4. AI errors and human responsibility

**Detailed Explanation:** AI can create inaccurate or unsupported statements that sound confident. The employee using the result remains responsible for checking facts, correcting omissions, protecting information, and following the existing approval process.

**Key Points:**

- An unsupported statement is not made reliable by professional wording.
- Review must check facts, omissions, tone, privacy, and approval.
- Important claims should be traceable to an approved source.

::: tip Real-life example
An AI-written appointment reminder adds a cancellation fee that is absent from the source. The reviewer catches the invented statement, removes it, and verifies the entire message before use.
:::

**Technical Example:** If a prompt provides three facts but an output contains a fourth factual claim, the review process should flag that claim as unsupported unless a verified source supplies it.

**How It Works:**

1. Keep the source available during review.
2. Compare each important output statement with the source.
3. Label claims as supported, missing, unclear, or unsupported.
4. Correct or remove problems.
5. Obtain the required human approval before use.

**Where It Is Used:** Every AI-supported draft, summary, chart explanation, call record, suggestion, prediction, or report.

**Common Mistakes or Confusions:**

- Checking spelling while overlooking factual errors.
- Assuming a detailed prompt prevents hallucinations.
- Correcting one visible error without reviewing the rest of the output.

## A practical definition

Artificial intelligence is software that uses patterns in data to perform a task. The task may be creating text, recognizing information, finding a trend, suggesting an answer, or following a learned pattern.

AI produces an output based on its instructions, available information, design, and configuration. It does not automatically know whether the output is correct, complete, current, permitted, or appropriate for a specific person.

<div class="concept-grid">
  <div class="concept-card"><strong>Generative AI</strong><p>Creates new text, images, summaries, or drafts from a prompt.</p></div>
  <div class="concept-card"><strong>Analytics AI</strong><p>Helps find patterns, comparisons, and trends in available data.</p></div>
  <div class="concept-card"><strong>Rules-based automation</strong><p>Performs a defined action when a known condition is met.</p></div>
  <div class="concept-card"><strong>Human judgment</strong><p>Checks context, consequences, permissions, accuracy, and fairness.</p></div>
</div>

## The human-owned workflow

<div class="workflow">
  <div class="workflow-step"><b>Input</b><span>A person supplies an instruction and permitted information.</span></div>
  <div class="workflow-step"><b>Processing</b><span>The system identifies patterns and generates a result.</span></div>
  <div class="workflow-step"><b>Output</b><span>The tool returns text, a chart, a suggestion, or another result.</span></div>
  <div class="workflow-step"><b>Review</b><span>A person checks the result against the source and rules.</span></div>
  <div class="workflow-step"><b>Decision</b><span>An authorized person decides what happens next.</span></div>
</div>

AI may support one or more steps. It does not inherit the employee's professional authority or responsibility.

## Story: the invented cancellation fee

Jordan needs a friendly appointment reminder. Jordan gives an AI tool the appointment date and time. The draft is clear and professional, but it adds a cancellation fee that was never included in the source.

The error is easy to miss because the sentence sounds reasonable. This is an example of an **unsupported claim**, sometimes called a hallucination.

### Think about it

1. What made the draft appear trustworthy?
2. What source should Jordan use to check it?
3. What could happen if the message were sent without review?
4. Which person remains responsible for the final message?

Do not use AI as the final decision-maker for care, eligibility, crisis response, diagnosis, legal interpretation, or another decision that requires authorized professional judgment.

## Interactive demonstration

Give the tool four confirmed facts about a fictional training event. Ask it to create a short reminder. Compare the result with the source facts and label each sentence:

- **Supported:** directly matches the source.
- **Reworded:** changes presentation but preserves meaning.
- **Unsupported:** introduces a new fact or assumption.
- **Missing:** leaves out information required for the task.

## Common mistakes

- Treating fluent language as proof of accuracy.
- Asking AI to decide when it should only assist.
- Assuming the tool understands missing context.
- Using the first output without comparing it with the source.
- Believing a better prompt removes the need for review.

## Mini challenge

An AI-generated summary says, "The team approved weekly outreach beginning Monday." The source only says, "The team discussed whether weekly outreach could help." Rewrite the sentence so it matches the evidence.

::: details Suggested response
The team discussed weekly outreach as a possible option. No decision or start date was recorded.
:::

2. Why can an inaccurate output still sound convincing?
3. Name two tasks where AI may assist but a person must remain responsible.
4. What should happen before an AI-generated message is shared?
5. What is an unsupported claim?

::: details Check your answers
1. Generative AI creates a new output; rules-based automation follows predefined conditions and actions. 2. AI creates likely patterns of language and does not independently prove every claim. 3.
