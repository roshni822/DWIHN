## Module Overview

This module consolidates the previous nine modules and asks learners to demonstrate safe, accurate, and role-relevant AI use. It is important because course completion should show both knowledge and practical judgment, not attendance alone.

::: warning Certification criteria require approval
DWIHN and Netlink should confirm required modules, attendance, passing standard, practical evidence, remediation, retakes, and certificate wording before launch.
:::

::: warning Schedule clarification required
The proposal describes the review as taking place in the final two sessions, while its detailed schedule assigns only Session 12 to Module 10. Confirm the delivery schedule before publishing learner dates.
:::

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module, learners can:

- Explain a complete human-owned AI workflow.
- Demonstrate safe tool, account, task, and information choices.
- Verify an AI-supported result against its source.
- Communicate limitations, uncertainty, and required approval.
- Respond appropriately when a concern appears.
- Reflect on performance and identify a next learning goal.

### 1. Reviewing the complete learning pathway

**Detailed Explanation:** Learners connect tool selection, data classification, prompting, output review, role-specific tools, evidence interpretation, governance, and incident reporting. The review uses realistic scenarios so learners must combine the skills instead of remembering definitions in isolation.

**Key Points:**

- Start with the business need and authorization.
- Protect information before entering or sharing it.
- Verify outputs against approved sources.
- Preserve human judgment, approval, and escalation.

::: tip Real-life example
A learner receives a fictional request to summarize a call and include a dashboard trend. Before starting, they identify the permitted tools, classify both sources, and plan how each output will be verified.
:::

**Technical Example:** A workflow record can connect task purpose, tool and account, input classification, prompt or query, source references, corrections, reviewer, destination, and final approval in one traceable package.

**How It Works:**

1. Present a scenario that combines several modules.
2. Ask the learner to select the authorized workflow.
3. Require a clear prompt, query, or review plan.
4. Compare the output with the approved source.
5. Explain handling, approval, limitations, and escalation.

**Where It Is Used:** The review prepares learners for final assessment and for everyday workflows that combine documentation, data, communication, and responsible decision making.

**Common Mistakes or Confusions:** Learners may focus only on prompt quality, forget input permission, verify wording but not numbers, omit the intended audience, or assume course completion removes the need for local approval.

### 2. Resolving outstanding questions

**Detailed Explanation:** The final review should surface uncertainties about approved tools, local policies, role permissions, data handling, and escalation routes. Trainers should distinguish a concept question from a local policy question and send unresolved local questions to the confirmed owner.

**Key Points:**

- Invite questions without penalizing uncertainty.
- Record unanswered questions and assign an owner.
- Use approved sources instead of trainer memory.
- Update the course when an authoritative answer changes.

::: tip Real-life example
A learner asks whether a specific transcript may be exported. The trainer does not guess. The question is checked against the approved catalog and the designated DWIHN owner provides the answer.
:::

**Technical Example:** A question log can include question ID, module, role, policy or tool owner, approved source, answer, decision date, version, and course pages that require an update.

**How It Works:**

1. Collect unresolved questions during review and practice.
2. Classify each as conceptual, technical, policy, or role-specific.
3. Answer from the approved curriculum or assign it to the named owner.
4. Record the confirmed answer and source.
5. Communicate changes to affected learners and materials.

**Where It Is Used:** This process supports live question sessions, office hours, trainer preparation, frequently asked questions, remediation, and post-course support.

**Common Mistakes or Confusions:** Trainers may guess to keep the session moving, confuse vendor documentation with local approval, give one role's permission to everyone, or fail to update old materials after an answer changes.

### 3. Completing the knowledge assessment

**Detailed Explanation:** The proposal calls for an online 20-question final assessment and also describes a 200-plus-question role-based bank. The final form should sample the course fairly and measure decisions as well as recall through realistic scenarios.

**Key Points:**

- Map every question to an objective and module.
- Use plausible choices that test judgment.
- Set passing, retake, and remediation rules before launch.
- Review question quality, accessibility, and security.

::: tip Real-life example
Instead of asking only for the definition of restricted information, a question shows a fictional screenshot and asks what the employee should check before using it in an AI tool.
:::

**Technical Example:** Tag each question with module, objective, role, difficulty, data class, correct answer, rationale, and version. A 20-question form can then draw a balanced set from the approved bank without over-testing one module.

**How It Works:**

1. Build an assessment blueprint from learning objectives.
2. Create and review scenario-based questions.
3. Assemble a balanced 20-question form from the approved bank.
4. Score responses using the confirmed passing standard.
5. Provide approved feedback, remediation, and retake steps.

**Where It Is Used:** The assessment supports course completion, knowledge checks, remediation planning, refresher training, and evidence that required concepts were tested.

**Common Mistakes or Confusions:** Weak assessments overuse definition questions, reveal sensitive details, contain more than one defensible answer, test content that was not taught, or issue a certificate without confirmed passing rules.

### 4. Demonstrating practical readiness

**Detailed Explanation:** Practical readiness means a learner can perform a role-relevant task safely, accurately, and transparently. A certificate should reflect only the criteria approved by DWIHN and Netlink. A strong model combines knowledge results with observed practical evidence.

**Key Points:**

- Use synthetic or specifically approved training information.
- Assess the process as well as the final product.
- Require source verification and correct human approval.
- Define remediation, reassessment, and certificate wording.

::: tip Real-life example
A call-center learner corrects a fictional summary, identifies the evidence for every correction, protects the information, and explains the required follow-up. The assessor scores the process, not just the final wording.
:::

**Technical Example:** A practical rubric can score authorization, accuracy, privacy, tool operation, critical thinking, human accountability, communication, and governance response as meets, partially meets, or does not yet meet.

**How It Works:**

1. Assign an approved role-specific capstone scenario.
2. Observe the learner's tool, data, prompt, and verification choices.
3. Score the evidence with a consistent rubric.
4. Provide feedback and remediation where needed.
5. Record completion and issue any certificate under the confirmed rules.

**Where It Is Used:** Practical assessment supports certification decisions, supervisor confidence, targeted coaching, onboarding evidence, and planning for continued supervised practice.

**Common Mistakes or Confusions:** Programs may score only the polished output, use real sensitive data unnecessarily, certify attendance instead of competence, apply different standards across assessors, or imply that a certificate authorizes unapproved AI use.

## Capstone: Community Access Improvement

The fictional Community Access Program has three visible patterns:

- Appointment completion increased in total but decreased as a rate.
- Call queues are longer during two weekday periods.
- A sample call summary incorrectly records a discussed appointment as booked.

The dataset, call transcript, meeting notes, and policy extracts used in this assessment must be synthetic and approved for training.

## Capstone instructions

<div class="learning-task">
  <h3>Demonstrate the complete process</h3>
  <p>Complete the selected role task and submit both the work product and a short decision record. The decision record shows how the result was produced, checked, approved, and handled.</p>

The learner must:

1. Define the task and intended audience.
2. Select the approved tool and account.
3. Classify each input and expected output.
4. Explain why the information and task are permitted.
5. Write the prompt, query, or review plan.
6. Produce or inspect the AI-supported result.
7. Compare important claims with the source.
8. Correct errors and restore missing information.
9. State limitations and unresolved questions.
10. Identify the required reviewer and approved destination.
11. Explain what would trigger escalation.
12. Propose one measure of workflow quality.

## Practical scoring rubric

Assess each area as **Meets**, **Partially meets**, or **Does not yet meet**.

### Authorization

The learner checks the tool, account, information, task, sharing, and destination. Any uncertain permission is escalated rather than assumed.

### Accuracy and source fidelity

The final product matches the approved source, corrects unsupported claims, and preserves required information.

### Privacy and security

The learner uses synthetic or explicitly approved information and follows handling requirements for inputs and outputs.

### Prompting or tool operation

The learner gives clear instructions, uses confirmed features, and records relevant filters, definitions, or workflow settings.

### Critical thinking

The learner separates observations from explanations, acknowledges uncertainty, and avoids overstating what AI or data proves.

### Human accountability

The learner identifies the responsible reviewer, preserves existing approvals, and explains when AI should not be used.

### Communication

The final product is clear, respectful, accessible, audience-appropriate, and transparent about limitations.

### Governance response

The learner recognizes a concern, records known facts, and uses the confirmed help or reporting route.

## Knowledge assessment

Use scenario-based questions rather than definitions alone. The assessment should sample:

- AI capabilities and limitations.
- Approved-tool decisions.
- Data classification and handling.
- Prompt construction.
- Output verification.
- Role-specific tool behavior.
- Evidence interpretation.
- Governance and reporting.

::: tip Recommended evidence model
Combine a short knowledge check with the practical capstone. A learner should demonstrate both what they know and what they can safely do.
:::

## Final reflection

Learners answer four questions:

1. Which AI-supported task can I now perform more safely?
2. Which check am I most likely to forget under time pressure?
3. Which situation still requires help or more practice?
4. Which measure would show that my workflow is accurate and useful?

## Course completion check

Before completion is recorded, confirm that the learner can:

- Choose an authorized workflow.
- Protect information before and after AI use.
- Verify a result against a source.
- Explain limitations and uncertainty.
- Preserve human ownership and approval.
- Use the appropriate help or reporting route.
