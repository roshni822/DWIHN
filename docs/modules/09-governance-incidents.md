# AI Governance and Incident Handling

<p class="module-lede">Governance makes responsibility visible: who approves AI use, who maintains guidance, who monitors results, and who responds when something goes wrong. Employees support governance by following approved workflows and reporting concerns promptly.</p>

## Module Overview

This module explains who owns AI decisions, how employees recognize concerns, what facts should be recorded, and how confirmed response procedures are followed. It is important because early reporting and clear accountability protect people, information, and organizational trust.

::: danger Insert the confirmed DWIHN process
The proposal refers to a five-step process and a 24-hour response but does not supply the governing procedure. Do not teach either claim until DWIHN confirms the steps, contacts, immediate actions, documentation, and timelines.
:::

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module, learners can:

- Explain the purpose of AI governance and their role within it.
- Distinguish an ordinary tool error, output-quality problem, policy concern, and suspected information exposure.
- Record useful facts without conducting an unauthorized investigation.
- Follow DWIHN's confirmed immediate-action and reporting process.
- Describe how lessons from concerns can improve training and controls.

### 1. The AI governance committee

**Detailed Explanation:** AI governance assigns responsibility for approving tools and use cases, maintaining guidance, monitoring performance, reviewing risks, and responding to concerns. A committee or equivalent group connects technical, operational, privacy, security, legal, compliance, and workforce perspectives.

**Key Points:**

- Name owners for tools, policies, risks, monitoring, and response.
- Review both the tool and its proposed use case.
- Keep decisions, conditions, and review dates documented.
- Give employees a clear route for questions and concerns.

::: tip Real-life example
A department wants to use an approved tool for a new type of member communication. The tool may be approved, but the new use case is sent through the governance review process before staff begin using it.
:::

**Technical Example:** A governance register can store use-case ID, business owner, tool, data classes, intended users, approved purpose, risk rating, controls, approvers, monitoring measures, and next review date.

**How It Works:**

1. A team submits a defined AI use case.
2. Relevant owners review purpose, information, risk, and controls.
3. The authorized body approves, rejects, or places conditions on use.
4. Owners monitor the approved workflow and record concerns.
5. The decision is reviewed when the tool, data, purpose, or risk changes.

**Where It Is Used:** Governance applies to tool selection, new use cases, policy maintenance, vendor review, access decisions, performance monitoring, and incident learning.

**Common Mistakes or Confusions:** Staff may assume an approved tool permits every use, treat governance as only an information-technology task, omit frontline users, or fail to review a workflow after a major change.

### 2. Recognizing an AI-related concern

**Detailed Explanation:** An AI-related concern is any event or output that may threaten accuracy, fairness, privacy, security, policy compliance, service quality, or human safety. Employees should report what they observe without waiting to prove the final cause or incident category.

**Key Points:**

- Notice unauthorized use, harmful output, exposure, and unusual behavior.
- Pause only as directed by the confirmed procedure.
- Record facts, time, tool, account, and visible impact.
- Report promptly through the approved route.

::: tip Real-life example
An employee notices that a generated report uses the wrong denominator and could mislead a leadership decision. The employee pauses use of the report, records the error, and contacts the confirmed owner.
:::

**Technical Example:** A quality concern can be recorded with timestamp, system, feature, input type, output reference, observed error, affected workflow, immediate action, and reporter. Sensitive details should be handled only as the approved process allows.

**How It Works:**

1. Notice a possible quality, policy, security, privacy, or harm issue.
2. Avoid unnecessary additional use or sharing.
3. Preserve the known facts allowed by procedure.
4. Contact the confirmed owner or reporting channel.
5. Follow authorized instructions and do not investigate independently.

**Where It Is Used:** Recognition is required during prompting, dashboard analysis, call-center assistance, report sharing, automated routing, access management, and any AI-supported decision.

**Common Mistakes or Confusions:** Beginners may hide an error, wait for proof, delete evidence, diagnose the cause themselves, contact affected people without instruction, or describe assumptions as established facts.

### 3. Following the confirmed incident process

**Detailed Explanation:** The proposal mentions a five-step process and a 24-hour expectation, but it does not provide the governing DWIHN procedure. The curriculum therefore uses recognize, protect, report, record, and follow only as a draft teaching structure. The actual contacts, immediate actions, documentation, and timelines must be confirmed before training.

**Key Points:**

- Use DWIHN's approved process, contacts, and timing.
- Take only immediate actions authorized by that process.
- Provide known facts without blame or speculation.
- Follow responder instructions and preserve relevant records.

::: tip Real-life example
An employee realizes information may have been entered into the wrong account. They avoid independent deletion, note the approximate time and tool, use the confirmed reporting route, and follow responder instructions.
:::

**Technical Example:** An incident record may include event ID, reporter, date and time, tool and account, information category, action attempted, output destination, people or systems involved, evidence references, and responder status.

**How It Works:**

1. Recognize the concern and stop unnecessary activity.
2. Protect information using only approved immediate steps.
3. Report through the confirmed DWIHN route.
4. Record known facts in the required system.
5. Follow responder directions through closure and learning review.

**Where It Is Used:** The process applies to suspected exposure, wrong-account use, inaccurate high-impact output, unauthorized sharing, biased recommendations, unusual system behavior, and other confirmed incident types.

**Common Mistakes or Confusions:** Do not teach the proposal's 24-hour claim or five-step labels as official policy until confirmed. Other mistakes include delaying for a perfect report, changing evidence, or treating a quality issue as too small to raise.

### 4. Manager responsibilities

**Detailed Explanation:** Managers create the conditions for responsible use. They reinforce approved workflows, make questions and early reporting easy, help staff reach the right owner, and pause work when the confirmed process requires it. They should not conduct an investigation beyond their authority.

**Key Points:**

- Encourage prompt questions and factual reporting.
- Protect people from retaliation for good-faith reporting.
- Escalate through confirmed owners and preserve required evidence.
- Turn recurring issues into training and control improvements.

::: tip Real-life example
A team member reports a possible mistake immediately. The manager thanks them for raising it, preserves the known facts, contacts the designated response team, and avoids blaming the employee before the issue is assessed.
:::

**Technical Example:** A manager dashboard may track approved training completion, open concerns by category, response status, recurring quality themes, and corrective-action owners without exposing information beyond authorized roles.

**How It Works:**

1. Listen and collect the employee's known facts.
2. Check the confirmed immediate-action guidance.
3. Contact the designated privacy, security, tool, compliance, or governance owner.
4. Support the authorized response without changing evidence.
5. Reinforce lessons and controls after formal review.

**Where It Is Used:** Manager responsibilities apply during onboarding, daily supervision, workflow changes, access approvals, quality review, incident response, coaching, and governance reporting.

**Common Mistakes or Confusions:** Managers may blame the reporter, promise an outcome before assessment, run an informal investigation, continue a risky workflow, share details too widely, or invent a response deadline that policy has not confirmed.

## Recognize different concerns

- **Tool problem:** the application is unavailable, slow, or not behaving as expected.
- **Quality problem:** the output is incorrect, incomplete, biased, or misleading.
- **Policy concern:** the tool, account, data, task, sharing, or destination may not be permitted.
- **Suspected exposure:** information may have been entered, accessed, stored, or shared improperly.
- **Harmful recommendation:** an output could lead to an unfair, unsafe, or inappropriate action.

The learner does not need to determine the final category before reporting. Authorized responders assess what happened.

## Story: the wrong tool

After submitting a prompt, Morgan realizes the selected tool may not have been approved for the information entered. Morgan is tempted to delete the conversation and say nothing.

Unsupervised deletion may remove information needed to understand and respond to the concern. Morgan should follow the confirmed immediate-action instructions, report known facts, and wait for authorized direction.

## Facts that help responders

Record only what is known:

- Approximate date and time.
- Tool, feature, and account used.
- Type of information involved.
- Action the employee attempted.
- Where the output went, if anywhere.
- People or systems that may have had access.
- Immediate steps already taken.
- Error messages, prompts, or other evidence, where procedure permits.

Do not delay reporting while trying to create a perfect account.

## Common mistakes

- Hiding a concern out of embarrassment.
- Deleting records or changing evidence without instruction.
- Investigating beyond the employee's role.
- Contacting affected people independently.
- Reporting assumptions as facts.
- Continuing the same workflow before the concern is assessed.
- Teaching an unconfirmed reporting deadline or contact.
